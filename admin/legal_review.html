These carry the most exposure: federal and Orleans-Parish-specific requirements the lease currently says nothing about at all.

HIGH Federal lead-based paint disclosure. Any pre-1978 rental requires a signed lead warning statement, the EPA "Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home" pamphlet, and disclosure of any known lead hazards, with records kept 3 years. Penalties run up to $21,018 per violation, plus a private right of action. New Orleans' housing stock skews old; the lease currently has no such disclosure at all, though its own ancestor template (NOMAR) does.
40 CFR Part 745 / 42 U.S.C. § 4852d — EPA Lead-Based Paint Disclosure Rule
HIGH Orleans Parish "Healthy Homes" registration. As of 2026, every rental property in Orleans Parish must be registered via OneStopApp and hold a Certificate of Compliance attesting to Minimum Rental Standards (HVAC, water, electrical, mold-free envelope). Non-compliance risks daily fines, subpoenas, and revocation of the right to rent the unit at all. The ordinance carries anti-retaliation protections independent of anything the lease says — an eviction motivated by a tenant's code complaint carries its own liability regardless of what §9/§11 otherwise permit.
City of New Orleans Healthy Homes ordinance, 2026 mandatory registration phase
HIGH Fair Housing Act — service/assistance animals. A blanket "no pets" clause does not override the federal duty to accommodate a disability-related assistance animal, regardless of lease language. §5 PETS currently has no carve-out — real exposure to HUD/DOJ enforcement and private suits, not just a drafting nicety.
42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B) — Fair Housing Act reasonable accommodation; HUD guidance on assistance animals
MEDIUM Louisiana smoke detector requirement. One- and two-family dwellings must have at minimum a 10-year sealed-battery smoke detector at time of lease. Not mentioned anywhere currently. Worth a clause documenting compliance and shifting ongoing testing/battery/notification duty to the tenant — which also strengthens the landlord's position under the notice-based liability standard used in §16 (see below).
La. R.S. 40:1581
MEDIUM Flood / hazard insurance disclaimer for tenant belongings. Not a hard statutory requirement for leases (Louisiana's property-disclosure statute applies to sales), but the lease's own NOMAR ancestor template has exactly this clause, and given New Orleans' flood history, its absence here is an easy, low-cost gap to close.
Cf. La. R.S. 9:3196 et seq. (sales disclosure, for comparison only — does not itself apply to leases)

Preamble / §1 TERMNo issues. Month-to-month auto-renewal on missed notice is a valid contractual override of Louisiana's default tacit-reconduction rule.
§2 RENT MEDIUM The $50 NSF check fee may exceed Louisiana's statutory cap — greater of $25 or 5% of the check's face amount (La. R.S. 9:2782) — on a smaller check (e.g. a partial payment). The statute also separately requires a conspicuously posted notice of the fee on the premises; a lease clause alone may not satisfy that.
§3 SECURITY DEPOSIT MEDIUM The 30-day accounting clock is tied to "keys returned + premises vacated." La. R.S. 9:3251 ties it to receipt of the tenant's forwarding address in writing instead — a materially different trigger. Aligning the lease's language to the statute's actual formula gives a cleaner compliance defense. Bad-faith non-compliance exposes the landlord to actual damages plus the greater of $300 or 2× the wrongfully-retained amount, plus attorney's fees.

§7 PARKING / §8 SUBLEASE MEDIUM Louisiana law (La. C.C. art. 2713) construes ambiguous sublease restrictions against the landlord. The clause conditions subletting on "written consent of Lessor" without saying consent may be withheld arbitrarily / in sole discretion — tightening that phrase closes an opening to argue consent was unreasonably withheld.
§9 DEFAULT OR ABANDONMENT / §10 OTHER VIOLATIONSNo issue. Rent-acceleration language is standard, though courts can reduce it if "manifestly unreasonable" (general contract-law point, low priority, not specific to this clause).
§11 WAIVER UPON TERMINATION MEDIUM — citation error The waiver of eviction notice is squarely valid under La. C.C.P. art. 4701. But the second citation, "La. CCP 2713," is wrong — Article 2713 is about a lessee's right to sublease/assign, unrelated to eviction notice. Worth fixing regardless of severity: citing the wrong statute undermines a document meant to be relied on in eviction court.
§12 WARRANTY / §13 OCCUPANCYNo issue. The casualty/force-majeure language already reasonably covers hurricane damage via "other unforeseen event."
§14 UTILITIES / §15 ADDITIONS OR ALTERATIONS INFORMATIONAL No statutory issue. Louisiana has no statutory landlord-entry-notice requirement at all — unusually landlord-friendly. Since the only real protection here is the vague "peaceful possession" doctrine, a broader, explicit entry clause (covering routine inspection, not just repairs) reduces litigation risk rather than increasing it.
§16 LIABILITY GOOD — already sound This clause's structure (no liability except for defects the landlord "knew or should have known" and failed to remedy after written notice) already tracks La. R.S. 9:3221 closely — real, deliberate legal work, not boilerplate. One independent limit regardless of wording: La. C.C. art. 2699 makes a warranty waiver void, no matter what the lease says, for residential defects that "seriously affect health or safety." Not something a rewrite fixes — a limit that exists on top of any lease language.
§17 SIGNS AND ACCESS LIKELY FINE Checked specifically whether Louisiana protects a tenant's right to display political signs (some states, e.g. California, Washington, do by statute). No such Louisiana statute found, so the political-sign ban is likely enforceable as drafted. Caveat: state law was checked, not New Orleans municipal code specifically — lower-confidence flag, not a guarantee.
